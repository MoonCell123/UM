\section{Diagnosing Foundation-Model Relationships for Fusion}
To understand why different foundation-model combinations lead to different fusion outcomes, we analyze encoder relationships at three complementary levels: global representation geometry, feature-wise statistical association, and task-conditioned encoder contribution and pairwise interaction.
We further examine how these relationships translate into fusion performance as the size and composition of the encoder set vary.
\subsection{Representation Geometry Reveals Structured Model Families}
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/um_fm/PCA of Foundation-Model CKA Similarity.pdf}
    \caption{PCA of foundation-model CKA similarity}
    \label{fig:pca_fm}
\end{figure}
We first investigate whether pathology foundation models occupy a homogeneous representation space.
To characterize representation-level relationships among pathology foundation models, we compute coordinate-aligned linear CKA between every pair of encoders and aggregate the resulting similarity matrices across slides Fig.\ref{fig:pca_fm}.
Each foundation model is represented by its CKA similarity profile with respect to all other models, and these profiles are subsequently projected into a low-dimensional space using PCA.

Each foundation model is represented by its CKA similarity profile with respect to all other models and projected into a two-dimensional space using PCA. The first two principal components explain 73.3\% of the profile variance.
The resulting projection reveals a structured representation
geometry rather than a homogeneous embedding space.
Importantly, the representation groups are obtained by average-linkage hierarchical clustering using \(1-\overline{\mathrm{CKA}}\), while PCA is used only for visualization.
The complete CKA heatmap and dendrogram are provided in
(Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:cka_heatmap}) and (Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:cka_clustering}).
The projected similarity landscape reveals that foundation models are organized into multiple CKA-defined representation families: some models form compact groups with highly similar representation profiles, whereas others exhibit more distinct and isolated positions.
The complete CKA heatmap and hierarchical dendrogram provide consistent evidence for these representation organizations, indicating that foundation models exhibit different representation geometries rather than a homogeneous embedding space.
The CKA/PCA analysis uses all 13 available foundation models to characterize the global representation geometry, whereas the subsequent Spearman and task-driven analyses use the representative five-encoder fusion setting used in the downstream experiments.
Notably, membership in the same representation family indicates similarity in representation geometry, but does not necessarily imply redundant or complementary task information or equivalent downstream contribution.
In this five-encoder setting, the weak and non-significant association suggests that CKA and feature-wise statistical correspondence capture different aspects of encoder relationships.
Thus, CKA should not be treated as a complete proxy for feature-wise alignment.

To assess whether global representation similarity corresponds to feature-level statistical association, we compute cross-feature Spearman correlations between each pair of foundation models using coordinate-aligned patches.
Let \(\mathbf{X}_{i}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{R}^{P_s\times d_i}\) denote the embedding matrix produced by encoder \(i\) for slide \(s\), where \(P_s\) is the number of aligned patches and \(d_i\) is the embedding dimension.
For the \(a\)-th feature of encoder \(i\) and the \(b\)-th feature of encoder \(j\), we calculate the Spearman rank correlation across aligned patches as
\begin{equation}
   \mathbf{x}^{(s)}_{i,a} = \mathbf{X}^{(s)}_{i}[:,a]
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{ijab}^{(s)}
    =
    \operatorname{corr}
    \left(
    \operatorname{rank}\big(\mathbf{x}^{(s)}_{i,a}\big),
    \operatorname{rank}\big(\mathbf{x}^{(s)}_{j,b}\big)
    \right)
\end{equation}
Each encoder pair produces \(d_i d_j\) cross-feature correlations for a given slide. We summarize the feature-level statistical association of encoder pair \((i,j)\) by averaging the absolute correlations over all feature pairs and slides:
\begin{equation}
    R_{ij}
    =
    \frac{1}{N}
    \sum_{s=1}^{N}
    \left[
    \frac{1}{d_i d_j}
    \sum_{a=1}^{d_i}
    \sum_{b=1}^{d_j}
    \left|\rho_{ijab}^{(s)}\right|
    \right]
\end{equation}
The absolute value measures the strength of monotonic association regardless of correlation direction.
Finally, to examine whether CKA-based representation similarity is consistent with feature-level statistical association, let \(\overline{C}_{ij}\) denote the slide-averaged Linear CKA between encoders \(i\) and \(j\), and let \(R_{ij}\) denote their mean absolute feature-level Spearman correspondence.
We collect all unique encoder pairs from the upper-triangular part of the pairwise matrices (\(i<j\)) and compute
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\mathrm{CKA\text{-}FS}}
    =
    \operatorname{Spearman}
    \left(
    \left\{\overline{C}_{ij}\right\}_{i<j},
    \left\{R_{ij}\right\}_{i<j}
    \right)
\end{equation}
This statistic measures the rank-order consistency between global representation similarity and feature-wise statistical correspondence.
We perform this analysis on the representative five-encoder fusion setting used in downstream experiments, which contains ten unique encoder pairs, we observe no significant monotonic association between mean Linear CKA and mean absolute feature-level Spearman correspondence (\(\rho_{\mathrm{CKA\text{-}FS}}=-0.309\), \(p=0.385\)).
Therefore, CKA alone may not fully characterize the relationships among foundation-model representations.

\subsection{Fusion Performance Depends on Representation Composition}
Although foundation models exhibit structured representation relationships, it remains unclear whether increasing the number of integrated encoders improves downstream performance.
We hypothesize that fusion effectiveness depends not only on the number of integrated encoders, but also on the composition and interaction characteristics of the representation pool.
To investigate this hypothesis, we perform encoder scaling experiments on TCGA-UVM\citep{uvmdataset} using D3 and M3 subtype\citep{fouruvm} classification as the downstream task.
Six baseline fusion methods and our CoR-Fuse are evaluated under progressively expanded encoder sets.
The results (Appendix Fig.\ref{fig:scaling_law}) show that fusion performance exhibits non-monotonic and method-dependent scaling with the number of integrated encoders.
Adding an encoder can improve performance under one representation composition but provide limited benefit or even reduce performance under another. 
Therefore, the number of integrated encoders alone is insufficient to explain fusion performance; the effect of an additional encoder depends on the composition of the model set and the relationships among the included representations.
Therefore, effective multi-foundation-model fusion requires understanding encoder relationships beyond model quantity.

\subsection{Sample-specific Encoder Contributions Motivates Adaptive Routing}
While representation-level and feature-level relationships characterize encoder similarities, they cannot directly reveal whether an individual encoder contributes useful information for downstream tasks.
Therefore, we further perform task-driven analysis to quantify encoder-specific contribution and investigate whether encoder importance varies across slides.
To quantify the contribution of each foundation model, we adopt a leave-one-encoder-out (LOO) attribution strategy.
For a trained reference classifier using the complete encoder set \(\mathcal{E}\), let \(f_y(\mathcal{E}(x))\) denote the target-class one-vs-rest logit margin for slide \(x\). 
For encoder \(m\), its LOO contribution is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    C_m^{\mathrm{LOO}}(x)
    =
    f_y\big(\mathcal{E}(x)\big)
    -
    f_y\big(\mathcal{E}_{\setminus m}(x)\big),
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{E}_{\setminus m}(x)\) denotes the encoder set in which the embedding of encoder \(m\) is replaced by a predefined baseline.
The LOO analysis reveals substantial slide-level variation in encoder contribution (Appendix Fig.\ref{fig:attribution_distribution}).
Different slides exhibit different top-contributing foundation models, indicating that no single encoder consistently dominates across samples.
To further investigate whether encoder pairs exhibit non-additive interactions, we extend the analysis to pairwise task-level interaction.
For a pair of encoders $(i,j)$, we define the pairwise interaction attribution as:
\begin{equation}
    I_{ij}(x)
    =
    f(\mathcal{E}(x))
    -
    f(\mathcal{E}_{\setminus i}(x))
    -
    f(\mathcal{E}_{\setminus j}(x))
    +
    f(\mathcal{E}_{\setminus \{i,j\}}(x))
\end{equation}
This inclusion--exclusion score quantifies the non-additive task-specific interaction between two encoders, where positive and negative values indicate synergistic and interfering effects, respectively.
The interaction analysis reveals that representation similarity and task-level collaboration are partially decoupled (Appendix Fig.\ref{fig:n5_high_redundancy_quadrants}).
For example, H-optimus0 and H-optimus1 exhibit high CKA similarity while maintaining substantial pairwise interaction, suggesting that similar representation geometries can still provide additional task-relevant evidence.
Therefore, representation similarity alone is insufficient to characterize encoder redundancy, motivating adaptive routing that considers sample-specific encoder utility.
