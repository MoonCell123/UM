\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\textbf{Datasets.}
To evaluate multi-foundation-model fusion under different downstream tasks, we conduct experiments on two TCGA cohorts, including TCGA-UVM\citep{uvmdataset} and TCGA-BLCA\citep{blcatp53mutation}.
We use 80 WSIs from TCGA-UVM as the primary benchmark.
Following previous molecular characterization studies, we formulate the task as D3/M3 subtype binary classification\citep{fouruvm}, with 38 D3 cases and 42 M3 cases.
We further evaluate our approach on TCGA-BLCA, consisting of 455 WSIs. 
The task is formulated as binary prediction of TP53 mutation status from WSI representations, distinguishing TP53-mutant and TP53-wildtype cases\citep{blcatp53mutation}, with 238 mutant and 219 wildtype cases.

\textbf{Implementation Details.}
For both TCGA-UVM and TCGA-BLCA datasets, we perform patient-level stratified five-fold cross-validation and report the average performance across five folds. 
All foundation-model embeddings are extracted using the same preprocessing pipeline.
Performance is evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC). Computational efficiency is compared in terms of the number of parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs).
Detailed training configurations and hyperparameter settings are provided in the supplementary materials.
\subsection{Experimental Results}
\subsubsection{Multi-Foundation-Model Fusion under Different Representation Compositions}
\textbf{Individual Foundation Model Performance.}
We first evaluate all 13 FMs on TCGA-UVM (Appendix Table \ref{tab:uvm_all_fm_performance}.), orderd by AUC and the results are reported as mean standard deviation over five folds.
The individual performance varies substantially across foundation models, suggesting that different encoders provide different levels of task relevance and motivating representation-aware fusion.

\textbf{Comparison with Existing Fusion Strategies.}
Based on the above analysis, we hypothesize that fusion effectiveness depends on the composition of integrated representations rather than the number of encoders alone.
Therefore, we evaluate representative fusion strategies under different foundation-model compositions.
We compare representative fusion paradigms, including concatenation, mean fusion, static global weighting, gated fusion and attention-based fusion.
Mean fusion assigns equal weights to all encoders, while static global weighting learns a shared encoder-level weighting scheme across all slides.
Gated fusion uses a shared linear gate maps each descriptor to a scalar score, which is transformed by a sigmoid function and normalized across encoders.
We fix the number of integrated foundation models to five and construct several representative encoder sets with different representation characteristics based on the CKA analysis in Fig.\ref{fig:pca_fm}.

The first composition contains highly aligned representations: \{H-optimus-0, H-optimus-1, UNI, GigaPath, Phikon-v2\}.
In this setting (Appendix Table \ref{tab:uvm_high_similarity}.), attention-based method achieve better performance than simple aggregation strategies.
The second composition contains more heterogeneous representations: \{Hibou-L, Virchow2, CONCH-v1.5, UNI-v2, H-optimus-0\}.
Interestingly, simple fusion strategies outperform attention-based methods in this setting (Appendix Table \ref{tab:uvm_high_diversity}.), suggesting that more complex interaction modeling does not always improve fusion performance.
Finally, we evaluate a representative composition combining diverse representation characteristics: \{H-optimus-1, H-optimus-0, Virchow2, UNI-v2, CONCH-v1.5\}.
As shown in Table \ref{tab:uvm_n5_balanced_group}, CoR-Fuse achieves strong and stable performance across this representative composition.
Overall, these results demonstrate that fusion behavior is conditioned on representation composition, and adaptive encoder routing provides a robust strategy across different foundation-model combinations.

\textbf{Fusion Performance with Different Foundation Model Set Sizes.}
We systematically evaluate different fusion strategies under varying foundation-model set sizes.
Specifically, we gradually increase the number of integrated foundation models from two to ten and evaluate six fusion strategies and CoR-Fuse.
The results in Appendix Fig. \ref{fig:scaling_law} show that increasing the number of integrated foundation models does not consistently improve fusion performance, and the benefit of additional encoders varies substantially across fusion strategies.
Additional encoders may introduce redundant representations or limited task-relevant information, resulting in marginal gains or performance degradation.
In contrast, CoR-Fuse maintains comparatively stable performance as the number of integrated foundation models varies, suggesting greater robustness to changes in encoder set size.

To simulate practical deployment scenarios where users integrate a limited yet diverse set of available foundation models, we select five representative pathology foundation models spanning different architectures, pretraining strategies, and model scales: \{UNI, Virchow2, Midnight-12K, GigaPath, Phikon\}.


\subsection{Ablation Study}
To understand the contribution of the main design choices in CoR-Fuse, we conduct ablation studies on TCGA-UVM under the representative five-encoder composition.
Table \ref{tab:ablation} shows the result of validating each module, it suggests that sample-specific routing provides a more stable integration mechanism than fixed or globally shared fusion strategies, although its benefit remains dependent on the underlying representation composition.

\textbf{Effect of Adaptive Routing.}
Compared with uniform mean fusion, removing adaptive routing indicating that treating all encoders equally is insufficient when their representation quality and task relevance vary across slides.

\textbf{Global versus Sample-Specific Routing.}
To distinguish sample-specific routing from simply learning a fixed encoder preference, we further compare with a static global-weight baseline. 
The static global-weight baseline obtains the same AUC as uniform mean fusion.
As shown in (Appendix Table \ref{tab:static_global_weights}) its learned weights deviate only slightly from uniform weighting, which explains why these small changes do not alter the sample ranking underlying AUC.

\textbf{Effect of Consensus Representation.}
Without the consensus signal, the router must estimate encoder importance independently for each encoder, lacking access to the relative position of an encoder representation with respect to the group-level representation.
This variant leads to lower ranking-based performance than the full model, demonstrating that the consensus representation provides complementary relational context for estimating encoder contributions. 

\textbf{Effect of Task-Driven Warm Start.}
To evaluate its contribution, we remove the warm-start stage while keeping the routing architecture, data splits, and downstream training budget unchanged.
These results suggest that the main benefit of warm-start is improved ranking stability rather than a uniform improvement at a fixed decision threshold.
